Armada

Private payments feature ideas

A prioritized set of payment primitives that could make Armada more useful for recurring organizational flows, onboarding, and private business relationships.
1

Private Standing Payment Mandates

Bob signs once to let a specific payer or platform pay him privately on an ongoing basis, within agreed limits. This is especially useful for payroll, contractors, grants, and other relationships where asking for a fresh acceptance every time would become friction.

Technically: Bob signs a reusable authorization that binds the payer or integrator, his receiving material, the asset and chain, a validity window, and optional amount or frequency limits. Future payments are then ordinary private transfers made under that standing mandate.

Key trade-offs: More policy and revocation logic; sender-side tracking may be needed for limits; mandate rotation has to be unambiguous.
2

Private Invoices / Payment Requests

Bob can send Alice an invoice or payment request that already contains everything needed to pay him privately. It fits naturally into vendor, contractor, grant, and B2B workflows, while making recipient onboarding feel like a normal payment step rather than a separate privacy setup process.

Technically: Bob signs a request containing the amount, asset, memo or invoice reference, expiry, and authenticated shielded receiving material. Alice verifies it and settles with a standard Armada private transfer.

Key trade-offs: Requires off-chain delivery; invoice metadata can leak through the transport; replay, expiry, and idempotency rules need to be tight.
3

Per-Counterparty Receiving Capabilities

Instead of Bob giving everyone the same receiving identity, he gives different payers or platforms their own reusable private receiving capability. That reduces cross-context correlation and lets the relationship itself become the privacy boundary.

Technically: Armada derives or registers a scoped receiving subkey for a specific counterparty or integrator context, then uses ordinary shielded transfers to that scoped public material.

Key trade-offs: Depends on whether multiple receiving identities fit the existing spend model without circuit changes; increases key-management and recovery complexity.
4

Reusable Receiving Profiles

Once Alice has successfully paid Bob through an authenticated intent flow, she can save him as a trusted private-pay contact. That turns the first payment into one-time onboarding, while later payments become nearly instant.

Technically: Alice stores Bob’s authenticated receiving material, its provenance, and a policy flag allowing immediate future transfers. Later payments can use the fast path unless either side requires a fresh acceptance.

Key trade-offs: Stale or rotated keys become an operational risk; the integrity of the contact store becomes security-sensitive.
5

Private Forwarding / Recipient Routing

Bob can receive a payment privately and then have it automatically routed somewhere else without telling Alice where it ultimately went. This is useful if Armada is embedded underneath payroll, treasury, or other apps where the recipient wants control over what happens after receipt.

Technically: Bob’s app or integrator receives the shielded note first, then performs a second private spend or unshield according to a recipient-defined routing rule.

Key trade-offs: Immediate exits can leak through timing or amount correlation; automation introduces delegation and custody questions; the sender must never control the final destination.
6

Claimable Private Payments

Alice can send before Bob has enrolled, and Bob later connects his wallet and pulls the value into his real Armada balance. This is the highest-friction-reduction onboarding primitive and makes sense for gifts, casual payments, grants, and “send first, onboard later” experiences.

Technically: Alice funds an ephemeral shielded note and gives Bob a claim envelope through a link, QR code, or message. When Bob claims, the note is transferred privately into his canonical Armada wallet.

Key trade-offs: The sender can generally revoke before claim; bearer links are stealable if leaked; wrong-address and dormant-recipient semantics are weaker than intent-based payments.
7

Private Proof of Receipt

Bob can prove he received a payment without revealing the note itself, his viewing key, or where the money later went. That could be useful for invoices, payroll records, disputes, or compliance without creating a permanent monitoring handle for the sender.

Technically: A future ZK receipt proves control of one of the outputs created by a settlement transaction, without disclosing which commitment is Bob’s.

Key trade-offs: Likely requires new proof or circuit work; the receipt semantics need to be defined very carefully; probably not a v1 feature.
Priority reflects likely value to Armada’s recurring organizational-payment use cases, not implementation difficulty.
