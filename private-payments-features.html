Armada

Private payments architecture

How an ordinary Ethereum identity becomes privately payable without making privacy infrastructure the user's first problem.

The problem we were trying to solve

How can someone already inside Armada pay a normal Ethereum address privately, without forcing the recipient to understand or adopt Armada before the payment workflow starts?

The original intuition was that the payment itself should drive onboarding. Alice should be able to think “pay Bob,” not “first convince Bob to create a shielded identity, then ask him for a special address, then send.”

Alice sends first → Bob has done nothing → Bob later connects → the payment appears

The cleanest version of that turned out not to work. Armada’s shielded identity is not derivable from Bob’s 0x address or even from Bob’s Ethereum public key. Armada derives its shielded keys from Bob’s enrollment signature bytes, so Alice cannot know in advance what Bob’s eventual Armada receiving key will be.

That led to two different payment designs rather than one universal “send privately to any address” primitive.

The core architecture

1

Pay Privately

Shielded Payment Intent

This is the serious-payment path. Alice proposes a payment, Bob connects and enrolls if necessary, and Bob supplies authenticated Armada receiving material before any money moves.

Alice proposes → Bob accepts/enrolls → Bob supplies authenticated receiving material → Alice settles privately

Why this exists: nothing gets stranded if Bob never responds, dead or mistyped addresses do not lose funds, and after settlement Bob owns a normal Armada note with no sender clawback.

Best fit: payroll, vendors, grants, invoices, treasury payments, and any workflow where acknowledgement and destination verification matter.

Trade-off: Bob must participate before the first payment settles. The design deliberately chooses stronger payment semantics over “send first” magic.
2

Send a Private Claim

Claimable Private Payment

This is the asynchronous onboarding path. Alice can fund a claim before Bob has enrolled, and Bob later connects and sweeps the value into his real Armada balance.

Alice funds claim → Bob does nothing yet → Bob later connects → claim moves into Bob’s real private balance

Why this exists: it preserves the original “the payment itself onboards the recipient” experience and works even when Bob has never used Armada before.

Best fit: onboarding, gifts, casual payments, low-friction grants, and other “send now, onboard later” experiences.

Trade-off: before claim, the sender generally still controls the ephemeral key and can revoke; a leaked claim can potentially be stolen; wrong-address semantics are weaker than the intent path.
3

Known Recipient

Repeat relationship

The first successful interaction should create an authenticated private-pay relationship. Once Alice has verified Bob’s Armada receiving material, future payments can usually skip the onboarding or acceptance round-trip and become ordinary immediate private transfers.

first serious payment → authenticated receiving relationship → future payments become immediate

Why this matters: the friction is concentrated in the first payment rather than repeated forever. This is what makes the architecture fit recurring organizational payments.

Implementation: retain authenticated receiving material plus provenance and rotation/expiry metadata. A more formal standing authorization can later add payer, asset, validity, or policy bounds.

Trade-off: key rotation, stale contact data, and compromised local contact stores need careful handling.
The product story: Armada lets you pay someone privately even if they are not already an Armada user. For serious payments, the recipient accepts before settlement. For low-friction onboarding, the sender can create a private claim first. Once the relationship exists, future payments can be immediate.

Features that build on the core

Private Invoices / Payment Requests

Bob sends Alice a signed payment request containing the business context and authenticated private receiving material. It turns the intent path into something that feels like a normal invoice rather than a privacy-specific workflow.

Technically: bind amount, asset, invoice or memo reference, expiry, and receiving material; Alice verifies and settles using Pay Privately.

Trade-offs: off-chain delivery can leak metadata; replay, cancellation, expiry, and idempotency need explicit rules.

Standing Receiving Authorizations

Bob can explicitly authorize an ongoing payer or platform to keep paying him using the same authenticated private receiving relationship. This formalizes the Known Recipient state for payroll, contractors, grants, and other recurring flows.

Technically: a signed authorization can bind payer or integrator, receiving material, asset, chain, expiry, and optional policy limits.

Trade-offs: unless later enforced cryptographically, amount and frequency limits are payer/integrator policy rather than protocol guarantees.

Commercial Settlement Records

An on-chain private transfer can be final without necessarily meaning an invoice, payroll obligation, or legal debt is complete. Armada can give applications a signed record tying the private payment to the obligation it was meant to satisfy and recording what the parties treat as completion.

Technically: bind a business object such as an invoice ID, payroll item, or grant milestone to the payment, then allow a recipient or integrator to sign a state such as confirmed on-chain, received, reconciled, or commercially settled.

Trade-offs: Armada should provide evidence, not declare when a legal obligation is extinguished. That meaning belongs to the parties, contract, integrator, and jurisdiction.

Per-Counterparty Receiving Capabilities

Bob could give different payers or platforms distinct reusable private receiving identities, making each relationship its own privacy boundary and reducing cross-context correlation.

Technically: derive or register scoped receiving material for a payer or integrator context, then use ordinary shielded transfers.

Trade-offs: worthwhile only if the existing spend model supports multiple receiving identities cleanly; otherwise recovery and key-management complexity may outweigh the privacy benefit.

Private Forwarding / Recipient Routing

Bob can receive privately and then route funds somewhere else without Alice learning the final destination. This becomes useful when Armada is embedded beneath payroll, treasury, or other applications.

Technically: receive the shielded note first, then perform a second private spend or unshield under a recipient-defined routing policy.

Trade-offs: timing and amount correlation remain possible; automation introduces delegation and custody questions.

Private Proof of Receipt

Bob can prove that he received and controls a payment without revealing his note, viewing key, or where the money later went. This could support disputes, payroll attestations, or compliance without creating a permanent monitoring handle.

Technically: a future ZK receipt proves control of one of the outputs from a settlement without revealing which commitment is Bob’s.

Trade-offs: likely requires new proof or circuit work, and “receipt” needs very precise semantics. This is probably later-stage.

The architecture deliberately avoids pretending there is one perfect non-interactive private-payment primitive. The first-payment path depends on the semantics the sender and recipient actually need.
