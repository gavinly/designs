Armada

Private payments feature ideas

A pressure-tested priority list of payment primitives that could make Armada more useful for recurring organizational flows, onboarding, and private business relationships.
1

Standing Receiving Authorizations

Bob signs once to say, in effect, “you can keep paying me privately using this Armada receiving material.” That removes the need for a fresh acceptance every payroll cycle, contractor payment, grant installment, or other recurring business payment.

Technically: Bob signs a reusable authorization binding the payer or integrator, receiving material, asset, chain, validity window, and optional policy limits. Future payments are ordinary private transfers, with any amount/frequency limits enforced by the payer or integrator unless Armada later adds cryptographic enforcement.

Key trade-offs: The authorization does not itself force the payer to pay or cryptographically enforce business limits. Revocation, key rotation, and expiry need very clear semantics.
2

Private Invoices / Payment Requests

Bob sends Alice an invoice or payment request that already contains everything needed to pay him privately. It makes Armada fit naturally into vendor, contractor, grant, and B2B workflows instead of requiring a separate “privacy onboarding” ceremony.

Technically: Bob signs a request containing the amount, asset, memo or invoice reference, expiry, and authenticated shielded receiving material. Alice verifies it and settles with a standard Armada private transfer.

Key trade-offs: Requires off-chain delivery; invoice metadata can leak through the transport; replay, expiry, cancellation, and idempotency rules must be explicit.
3

Commercial Settlement Records

A private transfer can be final on-chain without necessarily meaning the underlying invoice, payroll obligation, or other business obligation is legally or operationally complete. Armada can give applications a signed record that ties a private payment to the specific obligation it was meant to satisfy and records which event the parties treat as completion.

Technically: The payment workflow binds a business object such as an invoice ID, payroll item, or grant milestone to the settlement. The recipient or integrator can then sign a record referencing the payment and the agreed completion state — for example confirmed on-chain, note detected, received, or reconciled.

Key trade-offs: Armada should provide evidence, not declare when a legal debt is extinguished. The meaning of “settled” belongs to the parties, contract, integrator, and jurisdiction.
4

Per-Counterparty Receiving Capabilities

Instead of Bob giving everyone the same reusable receiving identity, he gives different payers or platforms their own private receiving capability. That makes the relationship itself the privacy boundary and reduces cross-context correlation.

Technically: Armada derives or registers a scoped receiving subkey for a specific payer or integrator context, then uses ordinary shielded transfers to that scoped public material.

Key trade-offs: This is only attractive if multiple receiving identities fit the existing spend model without circuit changes. Otherwise the privacy benefit may not justify the key-management and recovery complexity.
5

Private Forwarding / Recipient Routing

Bob receives privately, then automatically routes the money somewhere else without Alice learning the ultimate destination. This is useful when Armada sits underneath payroll, treasury, or other apps and the recipient wants control over what happens after receipt.

Technically: Bob’s app or integrator receives the shielded note first, then performs a second private spend or unshield according to a recipient-defined routing rule.

Key trade-offs: Immediate exits can still leak through timing and amount correlation. Automation also introduces delegation and custody questions, and the sender must never control the final destination.
6

Claimable Private Payments

Alice can send before Bob has enrolled, and Bob later connects his wallet and pulls the value into his real Armada balance. This is the most compelling onboarding primitive for gifts, casual payments, grants, and “send first, onboard later” experiences.

Technically: Alice funds an ephemeral shielded note and gives Bob a claim envelope through a link, QR code, or message. When Bob claims, the note is privately transferred into his canonical Armada wallet before he spends or exits.

Key trade-offs: The sender generally retains the ability to revoke before claim because they created the ephemeral key. Bearer links can be stolen if leaked, and dormant-recipient semantics are weaker than intent-based payments.
7

Private Proof of Receipt

Bob can prove that he received and controls a payment without revealing his note, his viewing key, or where the money later went. That could support invoice disputes, payroll attestations, or compliance without giving the sender a permanent monitoring handle.

Technically: A future ZK receipt proves control of one of the outputs created by a settlement transaction, without disclosing which commitment is Bob’s.

Key trade-offs: Likely requires new proof or circuit work, and the semantics of “receipt” need to be defined precisely. This looks like a later-stage feature, not a v1 priority.
Pressure-test changes: “Reusable Receiving Profiles” was folded into Standing Receiving Authorizations because it is more an implementation pattern than a separate product primitive. “Standing Payment Mandates” was renamed to avoid implying debit-style authority or protocol-enforced payment obligations. Commercial Settlement Records was added as a distinct business-layer primitive.
